Off-Policy RL for LLMs, Explained Visually

Importance sampling and score centering for policy gradient

Oct 25, 2026

    Part 1 derived the policy gradient for language models. A cartoon of on-policy training. The sampler is the trainer, so training heads straight for high reward.     Where off-policy data comes from

    Training is off-policy whenever the completions come from a distribution qqq other than the policy pθp_\thetapθ​ being updated. In LLM RL, an inference engine generates the rollouts and a training framework updates the weights, so we’ll call qqq the sampler and pθp_\thetapθ​ the trainer. Any gap between the two is called the training-inference mismatch. It is famously hard to get rid of, and it can quietly degrade training or collapse the reward entirely.Supervised fine-tuning, by contrast, happily trains on text generated by an entirely different model or written by a person, with no correction at all.

    There are three common causes of unwanted mismatch. Two come from timing, because the sampler generated the rollouts using older weights than the trainer’s current ones. The third comes from computation, because the two engines compute slightly different probabilities even from the same weights.

    Multiple updates per batch

    Generating rollouts is often the most expensive part of RL for LLMs, so trainers usually split each batch of rollouts into minibatches and take one gradient step on each. DAPO, for example, splits each batch into 16 steps. Only the first step is on-policy. After that first step, θ\thetaθ has moved, and every later minibatch comes from a slightly older policy.Classic PPO goes further and makes several passes, or epochs, over the same batch. Recent LLM recipes mostly avoid this for stability. TRL’s GRPO trainer, for example, defaults to a single pass. This staleness is a deliberate tradeoff for sample efficiency, and proximal policy optimization (PPO) introduced ratio clipping specifically to handle it.

    Stale weights

    To keep GPUs busy, large-scale RL runs inference and training asynchronously and serves rollouts with continuous batching. While the sampler is still generating a slow rollout, the trainer keeps updating the weights on faster ones that already finished. By the time that rollout completes, the trainer has moved on to a newer checkpoint, and if new weights sync mid-generation, a single rollout can even span several checkpoints. This staleness is worst in long agentic tasks, where some episodes finish in minutes and others take hours or days.

    Numerical differences

    Even with fresh weights and a single gradient step, the sampler and trainer rarely compute the exact same probabilities. They are usually different programs: after the sampler generates a rollout, the trainer runs its own forward pass over those tokens to compute log-probabilities for the policy gradient loss. Even on identical weights, the two forward passes can disagree for two reasons:

    • Different precisions or kernels. The two engines may run at different precisions, such as an fp8 or int8 sampler paired with a bf16 trainer, or a quantized KV cache.A floating-point format can store only a fixed number of values between consecutive powers of two, and every weight and activation is rounded to the nearest one. bf16 has 128 values between 1 and 2, spaced 1/1281/1281/128 apart, while fp8’s e4m3 format has only 8, spaced 1/81/81/8 apart. So the same weight, 1.3, is stored as 1.296875 in bf16 but 1.25 in fp8. They may also come from independent codebases, each with its own hard-to-detect bugs.
    • Different input shapes. Even with identical, correct kernels, the sampler decodes one token at a time while the trainer scores the whole completion in one forward pass. Different input shapes can change the order of floating-point reductions, and floating-point addition isn’t associative.Addition is associative when (a+b)+c(a + b) + c(a+b)+c always equals a+(b+c)a + (b + c)a+(b+c). Floating-point addition isn’t, because each intermediate sum is rounded to the nearest representable number, and representable numbers get sparser as they grow. In bf16, they are 1 apart just below 256 but 2 apart above it, so 257 has no exact representation and rounds back to 256. So (1+1)+256=258(1 + 1) + 256 = 258(1+1)+256=258, but 1+(1+256)1 + (1 + 256)1+(1+256) rounds to 256256256 at each step. When different kernels add a long sum of logits or attention scores in a different order, the final result can differ slightly for the same reason. Batch-invariant kernels remove the dependence on shape, but they take real engineering effort and cost GPU utilization. Running both engines in fp16 instead of bf16 greatly reduces the mismatch, but doesn’t eliminate it.

    All three sources of mismatch come from deliberate throughput tradeoffs. Multiple updates per batch, asynchronous rollouts, and fast inference kernels make RL practical at scale, and giving them up would make training much slower. So the practical question is how to train well on off-policy data.

    Sampling from a teacher

    In all three cases above, the sampler is meant to be a copy of the trainer, so any gap between them is error. Other times, the data comes from a different source on purpose, because it can show the trainer something it wouldn’t produce on its own:

    • A stronger model. Rollouts from a larger or better model can show the trainer solutions it would rarely find on its own. LUFFY, for example, mixes reasoning traces from a stronger model into on-policy RL.
    • The same model with extra context. Even the model being trained can act as a teacher if its prompt includes the reference solution or other privileged information; the student then learns from those completions without seeing the hint. STaR’s rationalization step works this way, giving the correct answer as a hint. Instead of generating completions, the teacher can also score the student’s rollouts.
    • Human demonstrations. Hand-written examples are off-policy too, and SFT trains on them with no correction at all.

    Here the gap between sampler and trainer carries useful signal rather than error, and the question is how much of it the trainer should absorb. The gap can also be large, which weakens corrections that assume a small mismatch. The rest of this post focuses on unwanted mismatch.

    Why drift compounds

    Distilling toward a sampler that never changes is benign. A frozen sampler: training settles short of the peak. Its target stays put, so ppp simply converges toward qqq and stops. But in RL for language models, the sampler doesn’t stay fixed. Every weight sync refreshes it from the trainer’s latest weights, and it then adds its own numerical bias on top. So the sampler is a slightly biased copy of the trainer that moves whenever the trainer does.

    That closes a feedback loop. A synced sampler: the reward peaks, then falls. Each step pulls the trainer toward the biased sampler, and the next sync copies those new weights back into the sampler, which applies its bias again. If that bias points the same way from one step to the next, each pull adds to the last and the error keeps growing. A quantized sampler could produce this kind of persistent bias: its rounding error is a deterministic function of the weights, and the weights change only a little per step (though nothing guarantees that successive pulls line up).We don’t yet know whether real numerical bias persists across steps. The trainer–sampler gap has been seen to grow during training, but kernel errors also look like symmetric noise, which would partly cancel. That may explain why off-policy runs often look fine for a while and then suddenly collapse.

    At scale, Modal saw this pattern training a 35B model on agentic coding. In every run that collapsed, the sampler–trainer KL had first grown threefold or more. Samplers matched to the trainer’s hardware and precision started with about a quarter of the mismatch, but GRPO still collapsed on them, just later. With the corrections below, runs stayed stable at nearly five times the mismatch. So the mismatch’s growth predicted collapse, and its starting size didn’t.

    Frozen vs. synced samplers

    Drift survives group centering

    Group centering, as in GRPO, works at the sequence level. It subtracts the group’s average reward from each completion’s reward, and every token in the completion shares that same advantage. Because those advantages average to zero across the group, you might expect the drift to vanish. At any token position, though, drift scales with the expected advantage at that prefix. That token-level average can be far from zero even when the whole group averages to zero.

    A prefix that has already made the “340 + 58” slip is headed for a wrong answer, so its expected advantage is negative. A prefix that is on track, like “340 + 68”, has a positive one. These prefix-level averages are largest in magnitude where the outcome is already mostly decided. There the later tokens barely affect the reward, so the signal is near zero and the update is almost pure drift.

    Group centering shrinks the drift, which explains why it is the most stable of the three schemes above, but it doesn’t eliminate it. Promising prefixes still pull the trainer toward the sampler, and mistakes push it away, even at tokens that don’t affect the reward.

    Importance sampling

    The classical fix for training on samples from the wrong distribution is importance sampling, a general Monte Carlo technique for estimating an expectation under one distribution using samples drawn from another.Here we use only the reweighting step, which some authors call importance weighting. Classic importance sampling also chooses the distribution to sample from, often to reduce variance. In RL the samples come from whatever sampler produced them, so there is no such choice to make. The RL literature still calls it importance sampling, and method names like TIS and MIS follow that usage.

    Token-level ratios

    Sequence-level importance sampling weights every token’s score in a completion by the same number, the product of all its per-token ratios. In practice, almost every method uses token-level ratios instead, weighting each token’s score by its own ratio rtr_trt​:

    ∑trt R syt,rt=pθ(yt∣y<t)q(yt∣y<t) \sum_t r_t \, R \, s_{y_t}, \qquad r_t = \frac{p_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t})}{q(y_t \mid y_{<t})} t∑​rt​Rsyt​​,rt​=q(yt​∣y<t​)pθ​(yt​∣y<t​)​

    Compared with the exact sequence ratio, this keeps one factor of the product and sets all the others to 1. Every token gets its own correction, but each term reweights only the choice of that token itself. It leaves the prefix before the token and the continuation after it (which decides the reward) uncorrected:

    The token itself is corrected exactly. With its prefix held fixed, weighting the sampled token by rtr_trt​ turns an average over the sampler’s next-token distribution into an average over the trainer’s. In particular, it restores the zero-mean identity from the recap:

    Eyt∼q[rt syt]=∑vqv pvqv sv=∑vpv sv=0 \mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim q}[r_t \, s_{y_t}] = \sum_v q_v \, \frac{p_v}{q_v} \, s_v = \sum_v p_v \, s_v = 0 Eyt​∼q​[rt​syt​​]=v∑​qv​qv​pv​​sv​=v∑​pv​sv​=0

    So the drift term vanishes at every prefix, and only the signal is left.

    The prefix and continuation are not corrected. Both still come from the sampler, which decides what prefixes the token is trained on (like “340 +”) and how the completion finishes from there to earn its reward. Correcting for those would require multiplying in the ratios of every other token, which is the product that explodes. So token-level importance sampling is an approximation. It works well when the sampler and trainer are close, since they then reach similar prefixes and finish them in similar ways.

    Token ratios are much better behaved than their product, but they are still unbounded. The largest ones come from tokens that the trainer finds much more likely than the sampler did. A token with q=0.001q = 0.001q=0.001 and p=0.01p = 0.01p=0.01 gets a weight of 10. Any one such token is rare, but a batch has millions of tokens, so some appear in every batch. A handful of heavily weighted tokens can then dominate the gradient. The weight never divides by zero, since the sampler produced each token and so gave it q>0q > 0q>0, but it has no ceiling.

    A gradient dominated by a few tokens causes three problems. It points wherever their scores point, so it swings from batch to batch depending on which rare tokens happened to be drawn. The two engines also disagree most on those tokens, often because of numerical error, so the gradient leans hardest on its least reliable parts. And a single huge weight inflates the gradient’s norm. If gradients are clipped by norm, as they usually are, the whole update shrinks, and every other token’s contribution with it.

    Score centering

    Importance sampling removes drift by reweighting samples, but its practical, bounded versions let some of the drift back in.     Score centering vs importance sampling

    Computed exactly, both methods cancel drift, but they get there differently. Importance sampling multiplies each sampled token’s score by its own weight, which depends on which token was drawn and can be huge for rare ones; practical versions bound that weight, and bounding lets drift back in. Score centering instead subtracts one fixed shift per prefix, the same for every token sampled there. Because it uses no ratio, rare tokens never get large weights, and there is nothing to clip. The two can also be used together.

Score centering treats the offset as fixed, the straight-through approximation from above, so here it approximates the sampler’s gradient rather than matching it.
    • Staleness (does not apply). A stale sampler runs older weights, so its offset, δ=z(θold)−z(θ)\delta = z(\theta_{\text{old}}) - z(\theta)δ=z(θold​)−z(θ), moves exactly opposite to the trainer’s logits and cancels any change to the current weights. The stale sampler doesn’t respond to the current weights at all, so it has no gradient for centering to match, and the sampler-gradient reading doesn’t apply.

    Computing the expected score

    For a language model, sˉ\bar{s}sˉ is a sum over the vocabulary, just like the softmax. With continuous or combinatorial actions it usually has no closed form, which is one reason off-policy methods there rely on importance sampling instead.

    Even for a language model, though, sˉ\bar{s}sˉ needs the sampler’s full next-token distribution at every position, while inference engines normally return only the sampled token’s probability. Logging the whole distribution is expensive. With a 152K-token vocabulary, one position takes about 600 KB in fp32, so a batch of 1,024 rollouts of 32K tokens each would need about 20 TB!

    One practical approximation logs only the sampler’s top kkk probabilities (the head HHH) and fills in the rest of the vocabulary (the tail TTT) with the trainer’s probabilities, rescaled to sum to one:Another option is a Monte Carlo estimate. At each position, have the sampler draw a few extra tokens (without continuing or grading them) and subtract their average score. That needs no tail model and is exact on average, but it adds noise, and a single estimate isn’t exactly zero even on-policy. Thinking Machines’ StableReinforce loss appears to do exactly this with 8 tokens per position, as Martin Marek found by recovering its gradients.

    As Modal points out, top-p sampling makes this exact. The sampler’s probabilities are zero outside its candidate set, so logging each candidate’s ID and probability (about 8 bytes each) gives sˉ\bar{s}sˉ with no tail to estimate, as long as the trainer applies the same mask to its own probabilities. The same log serves as the mask. It’s usually about the size of a top-kkk log, but it has no fixed size, since the candidate set grows wherever the sampler is uncertain.

    In PyTorch, the whole loss is a few lines on top of the usual policy gradient loss. It needs the sampler’s top-kkk log-probabilities and token ids, which vLLM and SGLang can return alongside each sampled token:These must be the sampler’s final sampling probabilities, after temperature, top-p, and any other processing, and the trainer’s ppp should use the same temperature. Inference engines don’t always return that by default. vLLM, for example, returns raw log-probabilities unless logprobs_mode is set to a processed mode, and returns at most 20 per token unless max_logprobs is raised.

    import torch

def score_centering_loss(train_logp, samp_topk_logp, topk_ids, sampled_ids, advantages, eps=1e-6):
    """
    train_logp:     [N, V] trainer log-probs (in fp32) at each of N positions
    samp_topk_logp: [N, k] sampler's top-k log-probs, logged during generation
    topk_ids:       [N, k] token ids of the sampler's top-k
    sampled_ids:    [N]    the token the sampler chose at each position
    advantages:     [N]    each completion's advantage, broadcast to its tokens
    """
    train_head_logp = train_logp.gather(-1, topk_ids)
    with torch.no_grad():  # no gradient through q or the tail's use of p
        q_head, p_head = samp_topk_logp.exp(), train_head_logp.exp()
        rho = (1 - q_head.sum(-1)).clamp_min(eps) / (1 - p_head.sum(-1)).clamp_min(eps)
        coef = q_head - rho[:, None] * p_head  # q_v - rho * p_v on the head
    sampled_logp = train_logp.gather(-1, sampled_ids[:, None]).squeeze(-1)
    centering = (coef * train_head_logp).sum(-1)
    return -(advantages * (sampled_logp - centering)).sum()  # normalize as your trainer does

    Without the centering term, this is the ordinary policy gradient loss. The reference implementation, in JAX and PyTorch, also supports composing with TIS and MIS.

    Quantized vs. stale samplers

    The previous sections make two predictions. If the mismatch acts like a fixed bias, as a quantized sampler’s might, most of the damage is drift, so removing it should be enough. If the sampler is stale, centering still leaves a gap between Covq\mathrm{Cov}_qCovq​ and Covp\mathrm{Cov}_pCovp​, so it may need importance weights alongside it. Two sets of experiments bear out both. Marek and Ryabinin test each kind of mismatch on its own and make it severe on purpose, so that the methods separate within a few hundred steps. Modal trains a larger model on a realistic mix of both.

    In Marek and Ryabinin’s experiments:

    • Quantized sampler.Quantizing the sampler is attractive in the first place because generating rollouts is usually the most expensive part of RL for LLMs. Lower-precision weights and KV cache make each decoding step faster and let the same GPUs hold more rollouts at once. On Qwen3-30B-A3B, vanilla policy gradient collapses once the KV cache is quantized, while score centering stays stable. At the most aggressive setting, with INT8 weights and an INT4 KV cache, score centering is the only method that trains well. TIS improves slowly and ends far behind, and everything else makes no progress, including PPO and DAPO, whose clip was designed for policy movement rather than numerical error.
    • Stale sampler. When the sampler syncs only every 64 steps, neither correction works alone. TIS collapses, and score centering oscillates. Combined, they do best, ahead of PPO and DAPO, which were built for exactly this kind of staleness.

    Modal’s run points the same way. They trained Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on agentic coding, fully asynchronously, with samplers on six kinds of accelerators at NVFP4, FP8, and BF16, so the mismatch mixed numerical error with staleness. Score centering alone and MIS alone each delayed the collapse but didn’t prevent it, and score centering’s run collapsed after its sampler–trainer KL had grown about threefold. Only the two combined trained stably to the end, centering the masked weights the way the next section centers capped ones.

    It isn’t settled why score centering alone oscillates with a stale sampler, but its logit gradient suggests a mechanism. A stale sampler with score centering alone: the trainer overshoots the peak and bounces across it. Centering’s push on each logit is set entirely by the sampler, and a stale sampler doesn’t know the trainer has moved. Between syncs, the trainer takes 64 steps computed for where it used to be, which acts much like one step 64 times as large. A step that large may overshoot the optimum, and the next sync may swing the trainer back past it, though as far as we know that hasn’t been tested.

    Centering also removes vanilla policy gradient’s accidental KL term, which pulls the trainer toward the sampler on prefixes with positive expected reward and pushes it away on prefixes with negative expected reward. With a biased sampler, that pull is toward the bias. With a stale sampler, it is toward the trainer’s own recent weights, acting like PPO’s KL trust region. StableReinforce keeps centering but puts a small pull back on purpose, a KL term toward the sampler with a fixed weight of 0.01, in place of vanilla’s VqV_qVq​. When the sampler is a stronger model or a teacher, that pull is how the trainer imitates it, so centering removes the imitation along with the drift and learns only from the rewards.

    Centered importance sampling

    Score centering and importance sampling fix different parts of the update, so they can be used together. Recall the split into drift and signal:

    • Bounded importance weights reweight the signal, but leave some drift. Exact weights reweight the next token by the trainer’s probabilities instead of the sampler’s, although the prefix and the rest of the completion still come from the sampler. Bounding the weights spoils this slightly, and also lets some drift back in.
    • Centering removes the leftover drift without touching the signal. It subtracts one fixed vector per prefix, the same for every draw. That makes the weighted score average to zero at every prefix, and leaves the signal untouched, since subtracting a constant doesn’t change a covariance.

    So stacking them keeps the importance-weighted signal and gets centering’s zero drift. Centering removes the drift that TIS leaves behind. In this cartoon, the capped weights still move where the signal is measured, partway from the sampler back toward the trainer, so both end up close to the peak.

    Fixing a stale sampler

    In the stale-sampler experiment, centering alone oscillated, while combining it with TIS did best. With importance weights, the push depends on where the trainer is again, so it slows down near the peak and settles. Centering alone pushes each logit by R (1yt−q)R \, (\mathbf{1}_{y_t} - q)R(1yt​​−q), which is set entirely by the stale sampler. Importance weights put the trainer’s probabilities back into the update. For tokens whose ratio stays below the cap, the weight pa/qap_a / q_apa​/qa​ undoes how often the sampler picks them, turning centering’s expected logit gradient ∑aqa Qq(a) (1a−q)\sum_a q_a \, Q_q(a) \, (\mathbf{1}_a - q)∑a​qa​Qq​(a)(1a​−q) into the on-policy form ∑apa Qq(a) (1a−p)\sum_a p_a \, Q_q(a) \, (\mathbf{1}_a - p)∑a​pa​Qq​(a)(1a​−p). It isn’t the full on-policy gradient, since the rest of each completion still comes from the stale sampler. But like the on-policy push, it fades as the trainer learns a token, even while the sampler stays where it was at the last sync. That may be why the combination is stable where centering alone is not.

    We don’t yet know whether these gains hold for long agentic rollouts under the milder mismatch of a well-engineered system.

    Wrapping up

    Everything in this post follows from one fact in part 1: the score averages to zero only when the model being trained draws the tokens. Off-policy, it averages to sˉ\bar{s}sˉ, and the policy gradient picks up a pull toward the sampler. Importance sampling removes that pull by reweighting each draw as if the trainer had made it, and score centering removes it by subtracting sˉ\bar{s}sˉ directly. Score centering alone works well when the gap behaves like a fixed bias. When the sampler is stale, though, its signal is out of date, and adding importance weights on top of centering helps by reweighting the signal toward the current trainer.
